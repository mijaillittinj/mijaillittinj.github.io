- We have the two fields we are after. - Now we fill the first block, the soot emission. - Its equation says each point emits according to the amount of soot, what each particle absorbs and the Planck curve. - We start with Planck.
- The emission block starts with Planck's law. - At the top, the furnace at 1073 kelvin and 1323 kelvin, each photo divided by its exposure so they share one scale. - Planck's law gives how much light a blackbody emits at each wavelength. - A blackbody absorbs all the light that reaches it, and the furnace almost is one. - C1 and C2 are constants. - As T rises the peak moves to shorter wavelengths. - At the temperature of a flame it sits in the infrared. - The camera sees only the visible strip. - The inset divides the curve by its value at 650 nanometres. - As it heats up, that curve rises less towards the red and gains blue. - That shape is what the camera reads. - Now, soot.
- By Kirchhoff's law soot emits at each colour what it absorbs, so we start with absorption. - It consists of spheres, the primaries, very small. - x\_p compares their size with the wavelength and stays well below one. - In the Rayleigh regime each primary absorbs according to its volume. - C abs, the cross section, is its shadow area. - E(m) comes from the refractive index m. - Kappa, the absorption per metre, is primaries per cubic metre times that area. - f\_s is that number times the volume of one sphere. - There are different models of E(m). - One is by Yon and co-authors, and other authors use 0.4 plus or minus 0.1. - Next, aggregates.
- The primaries stick together in open clusters, the aggregates. - The one on the left grows to 60 primaries, and N\_p is that number. - We use RDG-FA, the approximation of Rayleigh, Debye and Gans for fractal aggregates, branched clusters that look alike at every scale. - Each primary absorbs as if it were alone, so the aggregate absorbs N\_p times more, the line of slope one in the graph. - Kappa is still primaries times shadow area, and writing it with f\_s cancels the diameter. - That gives the equation in the box. - Scattering, light deflected without emission, grows with a slope up to two and matters little. - With this kappa we write the emission.
- With kappa, the absorption per metre, we write the emission. - Each ring of radius r and width dr has one T and one f\_s. - If the soot is at the gas temperature, the local emission j\_lambda is kappa times Planck at the ring temperature. - Its unit includes the steradian, which measures solid angle. - Hot gas emits in the infrared, so the visible light comes from the soot. - The graph is the spectrum of the ring, and the dashed line is that curve divided by its value at 650 nanometres. - More soot raises the curve and the dashed line stays. - More temperature raises it and tilts it. - So the shape gives T without knowing the soot. - Now we follow it to the camera.
- We follow the light. - In the cross-section from above, a pixel looks along the white line, the chord, at a distance y from the axis. - Ell is the chord length. - If kappa times ell is small, the flame is optically thin and almost all the light escapes. - Then the radiance L\_lambda, the light reaching the pixel, is the sum of j\_lambda along the chord. - The graph gives the fraction that escapes. - In the simulated flame kappa times ell is 0.20, and in the propane flame 0.15. - Kappa grows at shorter lambda, so green loses more than red. - More red arrives, and T comes out about 10 kelvin low on the axis. - That is self-absorption. - We do not correct it with LOSA, we report it.
- The light crosses the lens. - The sensor, a Sony IMX392, has a red, green or blue filter over each pixel, the Bayer mosaic. - Its curve eta\_j is the quantum efficiency, the fraction of photons that becomes signal. - We assume the Sony curve counts photons, and a watt of red carries more photons than a watt of blue. - So we multiply each colour by its wavelength, relative to a reference wavelength. - Tau, the share the lens lets through, is set to one. - That gives psi\_j, the response per watt. - The response above 700 nanometres matters in the colder furnace, which emits more infrared. - Next, the signal.
- From the raw signal S\_j we subtract the dark frame D\_j, taken with the lens covered. - What is left is proportional to the exposure time and to the light the channel lets through. - So we use the linear image and not a JPEG. - The constant is K\_j. - It gathers aperture, magnification, pixel area and gain, and the furnace measures it. - The histogram of one red pixel grows with the exposure up to 4095 digital counts, the sensor maximum, and then it saturates. - Its width is noise, so we average frames. - Pylon stores each value times 16, and the web tool divides it back. - Since the light adds up what each point of the chord emits, the integrals swap order and j\_j appears, the local emission of the channel.
- The top row is complete, and the bottom row undoes that path. - The dotted line shows that Abel is the inverse of the line of sight. - From the calibrated image P\_j, Abel recovers what each point emits, channel by channel. - We start with the projection.
- From above, the flame at one height is a set of rings. - The line is a chord, the path of the light to one pixel, which adds up what the rings it crosses emit. - That gives one point of P\_j(y), what one row of pixels sees. - There are two peaks because at this height the soot forms a ring near the edge, and the chords that graze it run along it. - This assumes three things. - The flame is symmetric about its axis and steady on average. - The camera is far away, so the rays arrive nearly parallel. - The whole flame is in focus. - Next we write this sum down.
- The flame flattens into an image with three channels, each scaled to its own maximum. - At the top is the calibrated signal P\_j, the signal minus the dark level, divided by K\_j and the exposure. - P\_j adds up j\_j, the local emission, along the chord. - The middle integral writes that sum with the radius, and the one below is the inverse. - Abel works with sums, and the ratio of two sums is not the sum of the ratios. - So Abel runs per channel, and the division between channels comes last. - The inverse uses the derivative of P\_j, which subtracts nearly equal neighbouring pixels and amplifies the noise. - So we move to rings.
- To avoid the derivative, we cut the flame into rings, with j\_j constant inside each one. - The row of the image becomes the column P\_j, one value per chord. - The matrix A has one row per chord and one column per ring. - Each cell is the length of that chord inside that ring, in pixels times delta y, the size of one pixel in the flame. - So the image is A times j\_j. - On the right are the rings seen from above. - The outer chord crosses one ring, the next one two, and so on towards the axis. - That is why half the matrix is zeros, and A is called triangular. - This shape tells us where to start.
- The first ring to compute is the outer one, because its chord crosses only that ring. - The box marks the first row of the matrix, with a single unknown, and we solve for it. - With that ring known, the second chord has only one new unknown. - We go on row by row towards the axis. - This is onion peeling. - It is the same as j\_j equals A inverse times P\_j, the equation at the top. - The column on the right fills with the values, and in the top view the purple shows the emission of each ring. - Now we add the noise of a real image.
- The graph shows j\_j along the radius, and the dashed curve is the true profile. - We raise the noise sigma from zero. - With noise we repeat the calculation many times, and the wide band shows how much it changes. - It grows towards the axis, because each ring carries the error of the outer rings. - To reduce it we use Tikhonov regularisation, which smooths the profile. - Its first term asks it to reproduce the image. - The second penalises zigzags between rings, with the weight alpha. - For each alpha we measure the misfit and the zigzag, and against each other they form an L. - Its corner is the best compromise. - Another way, pySAT, uses splines, smooth curves made of pieces. - ANNAbel uses a neural network, a flexible function fitted to the image.
- The steps in order, one line per channel. - We average N frames to bring the noise down. - Averaging is adding, which keeps Abel valid. - We subtract a frame without the flame, which removes the dark level and room light. - We divide by the exposure and by each pixel's K\_j, which drops towards the edges because of vignetting. - Step 3 rotates and scales with the ruler. - Step 4 is Abel. - Blue stops here, for lack of signal. - In step 5 red and green give T. - T is read after inverting and not on the image, because each pixel mixes the light of the whole chord. - Step 6 gives f\_s. - The exposure and the scale cancel in the ratio, so they only affect f\_s.
- We now have, in each ring, the emission of red, green and blue. - This block turns them into a temperature. - The ratio of red to green reads the shape of the spectrum, and the soot cancels. - We start with Planck in each channel.
- We start with the temperature. - The psi\_j curves come back. - On top goes Planck, scaled. - The thin curves are at 1500, 1900 and 2300 kelvin, and the thick one follows the temperature on the readout. - The coloured area of each channel is G\_j, what that channel receives per unit of f\_s. - As T rises, Planck tilts towards the blue. - The green area grows faster than the red one, and the blue faster still. - The bars show G\_j relative to red. - So the ratio G\_R over G\_G always falls as T rises, and each ratio gives a single temperature. - This is ratio pyrometry. - Next we write it as an equation.
- In the equation, each j\_j is f\_s times G\_j, and when we divide red by green, f\_s cancels. - The exposure cancels too, and only K\_R over K\_G is left from the furnace. - The table depends on how E(m) changes with colour. - At the base, the C\_2\* molecule emits green light, the ratio drops and T reads high, so we mask that zone. - Each ring reads the table backwards, from ratio to temperature. - We now have the temperature, the first of the two things we set out to measure. - Next we look at it in the two-colour case.
- With one colour per channel, lambda R and lambda G, each integral is the value at that colour. - At these temperatures the minus one in Planck can be dropped, Wien's approximation, and T comes out with a logarithm. - This is the classical formula, two-colour pyrometry, with E(m) at those two colours. - Planck brings lambda to the minus 5, and soot absorption adds one more lambda below, so energy gives 6. - The camera counts photons, which multiplies by lambda, and 5 is left. - With broad channels, the dashed line is the effective wavelength of red, the average of the colours it sees. - It drops as T rises. - So we use the table. - Now, the soot.
- With T in each ring, we move to the last block, the soot. - Each j\_j is f\_s times G\_j(T), so f\_s is j\_j divided by G\_j. - Here nothing cancels, so j\_j needs real units. - The calibrated image P\_j is a radiance, and for that it needs the absolute K\_j and the exposure time. - Abel turns it into emission per cubic metre, with A in metres through delta y. - The level of E(m) changes the soot, and its change with colour changes T. - f\_s depends strongly on T, because in the visible Planck grows fast with T. - RGB-ANNAbel starts with ANNAbel, and a second network gives T and f\_s per point, without the table. - The tool offers it, with red and green. - Then the calibration.

- The calibration holds if the camera sees the furnace the way it will see the flame. - The camera stays fixed and focused where the flame goes, and the furnace goes in front. - Focus, aperture and gain stay the same for both. - Refocusing changes the signal by the factor (1 + M) squared, with M the magnification. - As the furnace moves back, the image of the hole shrinks and its edge blurs. - Less light reaches a smaller image, so the plateau keeps its level and only narrows. - The flame is taller than the furnace hole, and the lens darkens the edges, the vignetting. - It is corrected with a flat field, a photo of something equally bright everywhere, such as a diffuser or an integrating sphere. - Next, what each calibration gives.

- Each block shows what it assumes. - The flame is steady on average. - The soot is at the gas temperature and its particles are small, as Rayleigh and RDG-FA require. - Almost all the light escapes, with a reported cold bias, and scattered light is neglected. - The sensor is linear. - The calibration fixes focus, aperture and gain, and the furnace K\_j holds in the flame. - Abel assumes an axisymmetric and centred flame, in focus and with parallel rays. - The temperature assumes one shape of E(m) and no chemiluminescence, and the soot also assumes the value of E(m). - If these assumptions hold, a colour camera can measure the temperature and the soot. - See you in the laboratory.
