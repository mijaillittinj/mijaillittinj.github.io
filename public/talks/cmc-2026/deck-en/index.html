Transport is a solved problem. The difficulty is the source term, which is a closure and is measured in kilograms of soot per cubic metre and per second.
Inside the source term there is nucleation, surface growth, condensation of aromatics and oxidation. Each one is a sub model with uncertain parameters.

Matching the peak soot does not determine the source term, because formation and oxidation errors compensate each other.
No instrument measures a soot formation rate directly, so it has to be inferred from other quantities.

This fixes the roadmap of the talk. Measure the soot volume fraction, the temperature and the maturity. The three thumbnails are the measured fields of the normal flame.
The burner has three concentric tubes. In the normal flame the fuel leaves through the centre.
Soot forms inside the flame front and, to leave, it crosses the front and oxidises.
In the inverse flame air leaves through the centre and fuel through the annulus, so soot forms outside the front.
Both flames consume the same equivalent amount of fuel, set by the oxygen available in the inverse one. They are two complementary laboratories.
A modulated LED at 660 and 810 nanometres crosses the flame and a camera records the image. The same camera measures with the LED on and with the LED off.
With the LED on, the flame leaves its shadow on the image. That transmissivity accumulates the extinction along the whole line of sight.
With the LED off the camera records the self emission of the hot soot, which carries the temperature inside the Planck function.
The images are integrated along the line of sight. The Abel inversion brings them to the local plane, where each point carries four measured signals.
Extinction depends on the amount of soot and on the absorption capacity of the material. Emission adds the Planck function, which brings the temperature.
Measuring at two colours gives the spectral slope of the absorption, which is the maturity exponent. Mature soot absorbs more flatly than young soot.
Each point has four signals and four unknowns, and every signal depends on several unknowns at once. The soot volume fraction and the maturity enter multiplied, and the Planck function amplifies temperature errors. The inverse problem is ill conditioned.
The network takes the coordinates and returns the four unknown fields, temperature, maturity, soot volume fraction and scattering. Each point of the plane enters on the left and leaves as four values.
The optical model turns the outputs into predicted signals, the loss compares them with the measurement and the gradient updates the weights. The physics enters through the loss.
There is no training data. For this audience it is a PINN, a global parameterisation with a physical residual. The precision is estimated by repeating the inversion a hundred times.
The normal flame concentrates soot on the wings, with a peak near 8 ppm, and the soot temperature lies between 1650 and 1950 kelvin. The soot volume fraction is on the left and the temperature on the right.
The inverse flame has a peak seven times lower, is colder and places the soot outside the flame front.
At the same equivalent carbon consumption, the two flames produce opposite soot structures.
Maturity translates into a graphitic fraction. In the normal flame soot matures on the wings, where it passes through the hottest zones.
In the inverse flame the soot stays organic and young.

First idea. The soot concentration does not determine the local rate. The peak volume fraction sits where formation and oxidation compensate, and two heights with the same concentration can carry rates of opposite sign.
Second idea. The spatial reconstruction of soot volume fraction, temperature and maturity, together with the transport equation and the velocity, gives the source term in both flames. It is a hybrid estimate, because the velocity is simulated.
Third idea. That source term is the variable that models predict. The calibration in progress shows that the model with literature parameters does not reproduce it and that combining the two flames improves the identifiability of the parameters.
