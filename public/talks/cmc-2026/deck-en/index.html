Hello everyone. I am Mijail Littin. With images of a flame taken at two colours and a neural network we obtain several properties of soot, and with them we answer a simple question. Where is soot formed, and where is it destroyed? [advance]
Let me start with the basics. What is soot? [wait for the animation] Soot is made of carbon nanoparticles. They are born where the flame has too much fuel, from large aromatic molecules that we call PAHs. Particles of 20 to 40 nanometres stick together into aggregates of a few hundred nanometres.
Why should we care? First, health. Soot is part of the fine particulate matter, PM2.5, that reaches deep into the lungs. Second, climate. Black carbon absorbs sunlight and is among the strongest warming agents we emit. And third, engineering. In flames and furnaces, soot radiation dominates the heat transfer, and aircraft engines now have particle emission standards.
To study it we use flames like this one, a laminar diffusion flame. [point to the burner] Ethylene comes out of the central tube and air flows around it. It is a diffusion flame because fuel and air arrive separately and meet at the flame front, this orange line. Soot forms on the fuel side. And it is laminar because it is steady and repeats itself every time. That is why we can measure it point by point and simulate it in detail.
This is the soot measured in one of these flames. On the vertical axis we have the height above the burner, in millimetres, and on the horizontal axis the radius. The colour is the soot volume fraction, in parts per million. It tells us how much there is, but not where it is born or dies. [point to the two circles] Is it formed down here, in the wings? Is it destroyed at the tip? That is the question of this talk.
In a combustion simulation, soot enters through this transport equation. The first three terms only move it from one place to another. The last one, in purple, is the source term, omega s. It is the only one that creates or destroys soot mass.
That source term is the sum of several processes. Nucleation, surface growth and condensation of aromatics add mass, and oxidation by oxygen and OH removes it. Each process is a sub-model with uncertain parameters, which is why the available models differ so much from each other.
Let me show you why this is a problem. Two made-up models, just a sketch. The horizontal axis is time as a particle rises through the flame, and the vertical axis is the source term. Model A, in yellow, forms soot quickly and then oxidises it strongly. Model B, in green, forms it more slowly and oxidises less.
But if I look at how much soot they predict, [wait for the animation] both reach the same peak. Compared only against that peak, both look right. The errors in formation and oxidation cancel out, and the peak hides them.
So the question is whether we can measure that source term directly. There is no sensor that does it. We have to infer it from what we can measure.
The idea is to walk the path backwards. In a simulation, at the top, the model comes first and the soot is the result. In this work, at the bottom, we start from the reconstructed soot, combine it with the simulated velocity, and the transport equation gives us the source term.
For that we need to measure three fields. How much soot there is, its temperature, which is coupled to the optical signal, and its maturity, which sets the density of the particles.
We work with two flames. In the normal flame, the NDF, ethylene comes out of the centre. Soot forms inside the front and has to cross it to escape. [point to the tip] There, in the region with oxygen, it is oxidised.
In the inverse flame, the IDF, it is the other way round. Air comes out of the centre and soot forms outside the front, so it escapes almost without being oxidised. Carbon consumption is the same. One shows us formation clearly and the other shows oxidation.
This is how we measure. An LED lights the flame at two colours, 660 and 810 nanometres, and a camera records the image. The LED switches on and off. When it is on, the camera sees the light that crosses the flame. When it is off, it sees the light emitted by the hot soot itself.
Each pixel adds up everything along its line of sight. Because the flame is axisymmetric, an Abel inversion recovers the value at each point, as in this map. In the end, every point of the flame has four signals, two colours with the LED and two without it.
How do these signals connect to soot? The absorption grows with the soot volume fraction and with its absorption function E. The extinction we measure adds a little scattering, alpha. And the emission is the absorption times the Planck function, which brings in the temperature. Mature soot, in orange, is almost flat and young soot, in blue, drops more. So two colours give the maturity, beta.
So at each point we have four signals and four unknowns, the soot volume fraction, the temperature, the maturity and the scattering. Everything is coupled to everything, and Planck amplifies the errors in temperature. It is an ill-conditioned inverse problem. [If asked: E is the absorption function of the soot refractive index, and it changes with composition.]
To solve it we use a neural network. It takes a position in the flame, r and z, and returns the four properties at that point. So the fields become continuous functions.
We have no labelled data. Physics is the teacher. The network proposes the fields, the optical model computes the signals, and the mismatch with the measurement corrects it. We repeat everything a hundred times to estimate the uncertainty, below 2 percent in temperature and below 30 percent in soot volume fraction.
This is the result in the normal flame. Left of the axis, the soot volume fraction, and right of the axis, the temperature. Soot forms a ring in the wings and reaches about 8 ppm. The temperature goes from about 1650 to 1950 kelvin.
And this is the inverse flame. Look at the colour bars. It has about seven times less soot, around 1.1 ppm, and it is colder, between 1300 and 1700 kelvin. All with the same carbon consumption.
The network also gives us the maturity, as a graphitic fraction. Young soot is organic and mature soot is closer to graphite. In the normal flame it matures in the hot wings. In the inverse flame it stays young almost everywhere.
And maturity sets the density of the particles. Mature soot is denser. Multiplying that density by the soot volume fraction gives us the soot mass, which is exactly what the equation transports.
Now we read the equation backwards. The flame is steady and we neglect diffusion, so the source term is the divergence of the soot mass flux. With axial symmetry it becomes this expression in r and z. It is a balance, what goes out minus what comes in.
These are the ingredients. The density, from the maturity, times the measured soot volume fraction, times the simulated velocity. [wait for the animation] And the result is the source term, blue where soot is formed and red where it is oxidised. No soot model enters the calculation.
Here is the normal flame with both fields. On the left, how much soot there is, and on the right, what is happening to it. Blue is net formation, red is net oxidation, and white is where soot is only transported.
[point to the blue circle] Soot is born low, in the wings and at the base. [point to the red circle] And it is consumed higher up, in the outer region towards the tip. And the soot peak, 8.1 ppm near 30 millimetres, sits exactly where formation and oxidation balance, not where most soot is formed. [If asked: it is oxidised by molecular oxygen and the OH radical, in the region the air reaches.]
If we rotate the field around the axis, formation is a ring at the base and oxidation covers the upper part. This is the source term that a simulation should reproduce.
To compare heights, we add up each cross-section. The yellow curve is the total soot and the blue curve is the net source. The net source is positive low in the flame, crosses zero right at the peak and becomes negative higher up.
Look at these two heights, 17 and 40 millimetres. They have the same amount of soot. But at the first one soot is being formed, and at the second one it is being oxidised. A concentration measurement would say they are the same. The source term tells them apart.
In the inverse flame the scales are much smaller. Soot forms far from the oxidising region, near the base, and is hardly oxidised. The particles turn blue and almost never turn red.
When we integrate, formation is of the same order in both flames. The difference is oxidation, 22 percent of formation in the inverse flame and 104 percent in the normal one. That is why one flame helps us fit formation and the other fits oxidation.
Now let us follow three soot particles through the normal flame. Each one starts where our measured data begin. [wait for the animation] On the right, over time, temperature at the top, maturity in the middle and the source term at the bottom, with blue for formation and red for oxidation. In 25 to 28 milliseconds, each particle heats up, matures and goes from being formed to being oxidised.
And this is what matters for modelling. A soot model receives the temperature and the gas chemistry, and it has to predict how much soot is formed and oxidised at every instant. These curves are exactly that, but measured. With them we can calibrate a model, which is what I show you next.
This part is work in progress. We take a soot model with parameters theta, solve it with a differentiable solver and compare its source term with the measured one. The adjoint gradient tells us how to change the parameters. And each point is weighted by the uncertainty of the measurement.
We tested it on synthetic twins, simulated flames where we know the answer. Only three parameters can be identified. Using the inverse flame and then the normal one recovers them, while a single flame leaves a 20 percent error. And adding the source term reduces the uncertainty by 9 to 24 percent.
This is a preliminary result in the inverse flame. On the left, the measured source term, and in the middle, the model with its literature parameters, on the same scale. The model is almost blank.
If we multiply it by ten, we can see its shape. The model underestimates the source term by about a factor of ten, puts it in the wrong place, with a correlation of 0.29. That discrepancy is what the calibration has to fix. [If asked: it is preliminary, only for the IDF, and the calibration is still converging.]
To close, three ideas. The first is that the soot concentration does not tell us where soot is formed or oxidised, and the peak appears where both processes balance. The second is that with the soot volume fraction, temperature and maturity, plus the transport equation, we obtain the source term in both flames.
And the third is that this source term is exactly what models predict. The ongoing calibration already shows that the literature model does not reproduce it.
