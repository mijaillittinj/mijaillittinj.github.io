This talk follows the path from an optical measurement of the flame to the soot source term, the quantity that combustion models predict. The picture is the soot measured in the normal flame, revolved about the axis and seen in perspective.
Soot is carbon nanoparticles born in the fuel-rich zones of a flame, from polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons. The primary particles, 20 to 40 nanometres across, stick together into fractal aggregates like this schematic.
It matters for three reasons. It is part of the fine particulate matter that harms health. Black carbon warms the climate because it absorbs sunlight. And in engineering its radiation dominates the heat leaving flames and furnaces, and aircraft engine emissions are now regulated.
To design cleaner equipment, the simulation must tell where soot is formed and where it is destroyed. That is still hard, and a measured field only says how much soot there is. That is the question of this talk.
This is how soot enters a simulation. Transport by convection and diffusion, and a source term that adds or removes mass.
Transport is a solved problem. The source term is the closure delivered by the soot model, and it gathers nucleation, growth, condensation and oxidation, each with uncertain parameters.
Two hypothetical models. One forms soot fast and oxidises it strongly. The other forms it more slowly and oxidises less.
Both reach the same peak soot. Matching that peak does not determine the source term, because formation and oxidation compensate each other.
No instrument measures a soot formation rate directly, so it has to be inferred from other quantities.
In a simulation the model delivers the source term and the soot field comes out. Here the path is reversed. With the reconstructed soot and the velocity, the equation delivers the source term.
For that, three fields must be measured, the soot volume fraction, the temperature and the maturity.
In the normal flame the fuel leaves through the centre. Soot forms inside the front and crosses it on the way out, so it is oxidised.
In the inverse flame the air leaves through the centre. Soot forms outside the front and escapes without being oxidised. Both flames burn the same amount and are complementary laboratories.
An LED at 660 and 810 nanometres crosses the flame and a camera records the image, with the LED on and off.
With the LED on, the camera sees the shadow of the flame, which gives the extinction. With it off, it sees the soot's own emission, which carries the temperature. An Abel inversion takes the images to the local plane, where each point gathers four signals.
Extinction depends on the amount of soot and on its absorption. Emission adds Planck and the temperature. Measuring at two colours gives the spectral slope, which indicates the maturity.
At each point there are four signals and four coupled unknowns. The inverse problem is ill-conditioned.
The network takes the coordinates and returns the four sought fields at every point of the plane.
An optical model turns those outputs into signals, the loss compares them with the measurement and the gradient corrects the weights. There is no training data. It is a PINN with an optical residual, and an ensemble of one hundred inversions gives the uncertainty.
The normal flame concentrates soot in the wings, about 8 ppm, between 1650 and 1950 kelvin.
The inverse flame has seven times less soot, is colder and places it outside the front, with the same carbon consumption.
Maturity is read as a graphitic fraction. In the normal flame soot matures in the hot wings. In the inverse flame it stays young.
The composition fixes the material density, and with it the soot mass that the equation transports.
We read the transport equation backwards. At steady state, without diffusion or thermophoresis, the source term is the divergence of the soot mass flux, evaluated point by point.
Three ingredients. The material density comes from maturity, the soot volume fraction from the experiment and the velocity from the CoFlame simulation. Differentiating their product gives the source term, a hybrid estimate in which no soot sub-model is involved.
On the left, how much soot there is. On the right, the source term. Blue is net formation, red is net oxidation and white means the soot is only transported.
It forms in the low wings and at the base of the axis, and it is oxidised higher up, all the way to the tip. The particles turn from blue to red as they rise. The concentration peak, at about thirty millimetres, sits where formation and oxidation balance.
If we revolve that field about the axis, formation is a ring at the base of the flame and oxidation a layer that covers the upper part and the tip.
We integrate over each section. The soot grows, peaks near 28 millimetres and disappears. The net source is positive low in the flame, crosses zero at that peak and becomes negative higher up.
Two heights with the same amount of soot. At one it forms and at the other it is oxidised, with similar rates of opposite sign. The concentration cannot tell them apart and the source term can.
The inverse flame concentrates formation near the base, in a compact zone, and oxidation is weak. The particles turn blue as they cross the formation zone and hardly ever turn red.
Integrated formation is of the same order in the two flames and oxidation in the inverse flame is much weaker. Above thirty millimetres the net source is almost zero and the remaining soot leaves the measured domain.
We built a differentiable solver with a two-equation soot model. The adjoint gradient fits the parameters against the measured source term, weighted by the uncertainty of the ensemble of inversions.
Before touching the real data we tested the method on synthetic twins. The model is poorly identifiable, the two flame sequence recovers the parameters and the source term adds between nine and twenty four per cent.
On the left, the measured source term of the inverse flame, with formation followed by oxidation. On the same scale, the model with the published parameters is almost empty.
Multiplied by ten the shape becomes visible. The model produces a smooth band where the experiment shows intense formation followed by oxidation. That discrepancy is what the calibration must correct.
Two ideas. The soot concentration does not determine the local rate, and its peak sits where formation and oxidation balance. With the reconstructed fields, the transport equation and the velocity we obtain the source term in both flames, as a hybrid estimate.
Third idea. That source term is what the models predict. The calibration in progress shows that the literature model does not reproduce it, and the two flames together make its parameters identifiable.
